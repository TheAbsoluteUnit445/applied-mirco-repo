Discrimination · 1/12

A group outcome gap does not by itself identify why the gap exists.

Job-relevant qualifications, selection and unequal treatment can all affect observed outcomes.

A theory of the mechanism and a research design are needed to interpret the gap.

Discrimination · 2/12

An audit study changes a group signal while holding qualifications fixed.

Bertrand and Mullainathan sent resumes to white-collar vacancies in Boston and Chicago. White-sounding names received 50% more callbacks, not 50 percentage points more.

Randomized names on otherwise equivalent resumes identify callback treatment in that setting. Callbacks are not hires, and the result alone does not identify every motive.

Employer taste can make a profitable hire feel costly to the decision-maker.

A prejudiced owner treats employing a group member as an additional personal cost.

Discrimination · 4/12

Customer and coworker prejudice can affect an unbiased employer’s monetary payoff.

Customers can reduce purchases; coworkers can resist collaboration or require compensation.

Competition need not remove these effects. The source of taste changes whether avoiding prejudice sacrifices profit.

In the illustration, λ is the weight on the individual score; one minus λ is the weight on the group mean.

At λ = 1, the group mean receives no weight. This mathematical endpoint does not establish that a real test measures every relevant skill.

Discrimination · 7/12

A qualification experiment tests only the information it actually adds.

In Bertrand and Mullainathan’s study discussed by Kuhn, stronger listed qualifications did not narrow the name-related callback gap.

That result weighs against inference about those qualifications as the main mechanism. Other unmeasured attributes remain possible.

Discrimination · 8/12

Expected discrimination can reduce the return to investing in qualifications.

If applications are ignored or extra qualifications receive little reward, a worker has less incentive to invest.

Lower investment can then appear to confirm the original belief. A group difference can be produced by the feedback process.

Discrimination · 9/12

Competition most directly pressures employer prejudice that sacrifices profit.

An unbiased rival can hire overlooked productive workers at favourable wages.

Entry barriers, customer prejudice and information problems can limit this pressure. Do not claim that competition universally eliminates discrimination.

Discrimination · 10/12

The recruiter’s decision environment and monitoring can reduce bias.

Time pressure and fatigue encourage shortcuts; the book suggests improving the environment so recruiters can assess candidates carefully.

Objective monitoring can improve decisions, as in the book’s baseball setting. Workplace practicality and whether performance measures are themselves biased still matter.

Discrimination · 11/12

Blind evaluation needs to preserve relevant individual information.

Screens improved women’s advancement in U.S. orchestral auditions while leaving musical performance audible.

Hiding criminal records can instead push employers toward race as a proxy. Kuhn’s ban-the-box example concerns young, low-skilled black and Hispanic men; it is not a universal result for all anonymization.
