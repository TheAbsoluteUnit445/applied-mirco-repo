Answers
4.1
a) Maximize w + eb - (θe2)/2 to e gives first-order condition b – θe = 0. Solving gives e = b / θ.
b)  The result is the same as under a). The worker’s income will never be in the ‘loss range’ and so the
R plays no role.
c) Maximize w + b/2 + e(b/2) – (1-e)R(b/2) - (θe2)/2 to e gives first-order condition b/2 + R(b/2) – θe =
0. Solving gives e = (0.5+0.5R)b / θ. Note: if the worker is not loss averse (i.e. R=1), the outcome is the
same as under a and b. If the worker is loss averse, he works more under this scheme.
d) Perhaps by paying w + b combined with the obligation to repay b when output is low. If the
reference wage in that case would become w + b, then the worker chooses effort equal to Rb/ θ.
e) The personnel economist is right. Any effort increase due to such contracts goes hand in hand with
an increase in compensation for employers operating in competitive labor markets. Moreover, such
contracts can also make that workers sometimes end up in the loss domain, which causes additional
utility loss, which need to be compensated by the employer in order to remain an attractive
employer. Note that these effects are not present when the worker earns a rent, e.g. due to a high
minimum wage or due to collective wage bargaining.

4.2 (copied from Peter Kuhn’s teaching material)
False. Even perfectly “rational” classic economic agents may value utility today more than utility in
the future i.e. their discount factor, δ, can be less than one.  This is because all sorts of uncertainty
exists, and our lives are finite in any case: you are less likely to be alive at dates further in the future
than at more recent dates so it make sense to place less emphasis on utility at later dates.
Present bias (β<1) refers to an additional type of discounting that places a lower value on any
consumption that does not occur right now in the present. The key difference this causes is that
present-biased individuals will change their plans simply because today has arrived, despite not
learning any new information that should change their plans. In contrast, traditional rational
economic agents will make time-consistent decisions, and will not change their behavior unless new
information comes up.

4.3
a) Contract A dominates contract B if contract A for any realization of output or the state of nature is
at least as good and sometimes better than contract B.
b) The original contract is strictly better up to Q=X and identical for Q>X. Hence, the original contract
dominates the other contract.
c) It may work as a commitment device. With the original contract, a present-biased worker chooses
too low effort from the perspective of the present self. The dominated contract introduces a penalty
for slacking off, inducing the worker to work hard, which is in the interest of present self who
chooses the contract.

4.5
a) The utility function U = Y – V(E), where V′>0 and Vʺ>0 has constant marginal utility of income
(because utility is linear in income.)  The utility function U = H(Y) – V(E), where H′>0, Hʺ<0,  V′>0 and
Vʺ>0 has diminishing marginal utility of income because the marginal utility of income, H′, is
diminishing with income.
b) Income effects will arise when we use a utility function with diminishing marginal utility of income.
In contrast, U = Y – V(E) is a utility function that has no income effects.
c) The first-order condition with the utility function U = Y – V(E) is:
b – V’(E) = 0
Hence, base salary a has no influence on E. And the bonus b increases E, because the marginal
benefit from effort increase with the bonus.
The first-order condition with the utility function U = H(Y) – V(E) is:
bH’(a+ bE) – V’(E) = 0
Hence, a decreases E, because a higher base salary means that the marginal utility from income is
lower and hence working more has less of an effect on utility from income. The effect of the bonus
becomes indeterminate: A higher bonus increases the marginal benefit from effort through the first
b (outside the function H’()), which means that each unit of effort results in a higher increase in
income when the bonus is higher, and hence more effort is optimal. On the other hand, for a given
level of effort, an increase in the bonus increases income, and so decreased H’(), and so makes
working less an optimal response. The sum of these two effects can be positive or negative. Hence,
we can have a “backward bending effort curve”.
