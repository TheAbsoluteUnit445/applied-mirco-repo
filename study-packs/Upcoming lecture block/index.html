How to study the block

Start each module by identifying who chooses what. A tax authority selects rates, a voter compares policies, a hiring manager predicts or evaluates workers, tournament contestants choose effort, and team members choose effort or membership. Some models deliberately hold income or effort fixed. Changing that assumption changes the answer.

For the next scheduled material, study tournaments and discrimination first, with taxation revision alongside them. Before 13 October, work through political voting and practise the original F24 median problem plus the book's cycling examples. Before 14 October, learn the team sharing wedge and solve F24 Q2, then extend to enforcement, production technology and selection. These are sequencing suggestions within this block; they do not replace a whole-course revision plan.

For each module, read the theory, close the notes, complete the prerequisite check and original question, then compare your solution with the worked explanation. Redo the incorrect step and finish with a new numerical variant. In verbal questions, give the actual causal chain in the requested sentence cap. A name such as “median voter,” “free riding,” or “statistical discrimination” is not a complete explanation.

Connections that help without merging the models

  • Private vs social marginal benefit: the Ramsey condition allocates revenue raising by marginal welfare cost; a team member captures only a share of a marginal output gain; a saboteur raises rank while reducing output. State which objective you are differentiating.
  • Information changes incentives: political sponsors may not know bureau costs; hiring managers may have noisy signals; tournament pay uses ordinal performance. Information problems differ across models and do not justify importing one model's result into another.
  • Transfers vs welfare: taxes and wages transfer money. Lost transactions, effort costs, sabotage and real lobbying resources can reduce surplus. A transfer can also change weighted welfare when recipients and payers have different marginal utility of income.
  • Equilibrium vs efficiency vs preference: a stable majority winner need not be efficient; a privately optimal team effort need not maximise joint utility; an efficient rule need not benefit each member. Test each claim using its own criterion.
  • Corners and kinks: bounded probabilities, no-work decisions, nonnegative prizes and income-tax thresholds can put the optimum at a boundary. A zero derivative is a tool for an interior candidate, not a universal rule.
