Team production and team pay are separate choices.

People can produce together while receiving individual incentives, if contributions are contractible.

The special incentive problem arises when rewards depend on joint output but effort costs belong to individuals.

Teams · 4/19

Budget balance prevents every worker from receiving the entire marginal team output.

If every member’s reward rose by one euro when total output rose by one euro, total rewards would rise by N euros.

For N > 1 this cannot be financed solely by that extra euro. An external residual claimant or other mechanism changes the constraint.

Repeated interaction can punish free riding when future cooperation is valuable.

Peers who observe poor effort may withdraw cooperation or impose a social penalty.

The immediate gain from shirking must be smaller than the discounted future loss. Observability and credible punishment are essential.

Teams · 7/19

Team pay can raise effort while reducing the worker’s utility.

In the UCSB study-room experiment, known-partner team incentives raised visits despite weaker monetary incentives. Anonymous partners removed that advantage.

When given a choice, 97% selected individual incentives. Peer pressure can make recruiting harder even when output rises.

Teams · 11/19

A weakest-link task makes each worker decisive when all colleagues work.

Binary effort Eᵢ ∈ {0,1}, output D min(Eᵢ), equal sharing and cost Eᵢ²/2. D is team output when all work.

For N > 1 and D/N > 1/2, both all-work and all-shirk are equilibria. Communication can change expectations but is not guaranteed to select cooperation.

Teams · 12/19

A perfect-substitutes task creates a volunteer’s dilemma.

Binary efforts and Q = D max(Eᵢ). One person can do the entire task; additional volunteers duplicate costly effort.

If D/N > 1/2, pure equilibria have exactly one volunteer. All-shirk is not a pure equilibrium, but uncertainty about who volunteers can cause delay or failure.

Teams · 13/19

Appropriate unequal rewards can create a leader under complementarity.

Book example: boxes F(n) = 20, 40, 65, 100; work costs 90. Per-box pay rates 5, 4, 3 sum to price 12.

The high-paid agent works even alone; that changes expectations and makes the next agents’ participation profitable. This relies on increasing marginal output.

Teams · 14/19

Ability differences and team size require separate assumptions.

With the same complementary technology and equal pay, effort costs 70, 90, 110 create a low-cost leader. This differs from an ability gap in a tournament.

With fixed full effort, choose team size to maximize output per member. Endogenous shirking can make a smaller team preferable.

Teams · 15/19

Team selection holds effort fixed and compares ability with mean ability.

Chapter 27 fixes each effort at one. Equal sharing pays mean ability d̄; solo work pays own ability dᵢ.

Below-average applicants want to join, but incumbents prefer above-average entrants. Effort incentives and selection are different models.

Diverse knowledge helps only if teammates actually share it.

Communication, social sensitivity and psychological safety matter. The book’s group-performance findings are setting-specific, not universal causal rules.
