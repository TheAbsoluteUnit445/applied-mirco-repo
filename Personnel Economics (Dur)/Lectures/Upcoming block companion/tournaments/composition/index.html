A promotion prize rewards rank rather than the amount of output.

The employer commits to a salary and a prize spread before workers choose effort.

Each worker’s probability of winning depends on effort relative to the rival’s effort.

Competition and tournaments · 6/21

An efficient prize makes private marginal effort incentives match social marginal value.

In Kuhn’s baseline, output already measures net revenue: social marginal value of effort is d.

Efficient effort is E = d. Equating it with Sd/R gives S = R, subject to global incentive and participation checks.

Risk-neutral tournaments and piece rates can implement the same baseline outcome.

A piece rate b induces effort bd; an interior tournament spread S induces Sd/R.

Competition and tournaments · 8/21

More competitors and later stages change the incentive problem.

Adding contestants lowers each equal worker’s level chance of winning but can strengthen competition. Incentives depend on the marginal chance, so the effort effect is ambiguous.

Interim luck can turn an initially even contest into an uneven one. Feedback revealing a huge lead can induce the leader to coast and the laggard to give up.

Common shocks disappear from a performance difference.

Relative pay can reduce exposure to common noise, but independent noise and the risk of losing the prize remain.

Competition and tournaments · 10/21

Tournaments can reward sabotage even when it destroys total output.

Lowering a rival’s measured performance raises one’s rank without raising useful production.

The incentive is private; the lost production is a social cost. Cooperation-friendly tasks can therefore be poor candidates for tournaments.

Competition and tournaments · 11/21

Collusion can lower effort without changing symmetric winning chances.

If both workers reduce effort by the same amount, their effort gap remains unchanged.

They save effort costs, while the employer loses output. Secret unilateral deviation and monitoring determine whether the agreement lasts.

A rank prize can make extra risk attractive to a trailing contestant.

A worker who is unlikely to win under a safe strategy may prefer a risky chance to overtake.

Expected output need not increase. Rank incentives and social value can diverge.

Competition and tournaments · 13/21

Uneven ability can weaken effort incentives on both sides.

A large ability gap can make winning almost certain for one worker and almost impossible for the other.

Handicaps or sorting can make winning more responsive to effort. Fair rules and symmetric competitors are different concepts.

Competition and tournaments · 14/21

Promotion ladders can reverse the one-shot case for equal ability groups.

An early promotion carries option value: access to later prizes. Mixed pools can increase this value for stronger workers by giving them weaker final opponents.

In market-based tournaments, an upset reveals ability and changes outside wage offers. In pure selection contests, informative comparisons can matter more than effort.
