Exercise 1.2
A company employs one worker. The worker has the following utility function:
U = w – ½Θe2
where U is the utility of the worker, w is the worker's income, and e is the level of effort
exerted by the worker. The parameter θ are both greater than zero. The worker aims to
maximize utility. The worker's income w consists of the legal minimum wage w* and a bonus
per unit of output equal to b. The production function is q=κe. The income of the worker
thus is w=w* + bκe. Throughout we assume that the worker is better off with this job than
with any other job or without a job.

a) Derive the level of effort that the worker chooses.

Each unit of output yields revenues equal to p for the company. The company aims for
maximum profits.

b) At what level of b does the firm maximize its profits?

c) Suppose the government decides to implement a tax on profits equal to τ%. The company
continues and the worker remains at the company. The company aims for maximum after-
tax profits. How does the profit-maximizing level of the bonus b change? Interpret your
finding.

Exercise 1.5 (taken from the midterm of 2022)
A company employs one worker. The worker has the following utility function:
U = w + γe – e2
where U is the utility of the worker, w is the worker's income, e is the level of effort exerted
by the worker, and γ is a positive parameter. The worker aims to maximize utility. The
worker's income w consists of the legal minimum wage w* and a bonus per unit of output
equal to b. The production function is q=e. The income of the worker thus is w=w* + be.
Throughout we assume that the worker is better off with this job than with any other job or
without a job.
a) Derive the effort level that maximizes the worker’s utility.
b) Give one possible interpretation of the second term of the utility function (that is: γe).
Write in plain words what this term may mean.
Profits of the company equal the production level q times the price of the product p minus
the income of the worker minus energy costs:

Π = pq - w* - bq – cq
where Π denotes profits and c denotes the energy cost per unit of output.
c) Derive the level of the bonus b that maximizes the company’s profits. Explain in a clear
sentence why the profit-maximizing bonus is lower when the energy cost c is higher.

1.2
a) Differentiating the worker's utility w*+ bκe - (1/2)θe² to e and setting equal to zero gives
the first-order condition for maximum utility: bκ-θe=0. Solving for e gives e=(bκ)/θ.

b) Profits equal pq-w. Substituting the production function and the definition of the wage
gives: pκe-w* - bκe. Substituting the effort level from the first part of this exercise e=(bκ/θ)
gives pκ(bκ/θ)-w*-bκ(bκ/θ). Differentiating to b and setting equal to zero gives the first-
order condition for maximum profits: pκ(κ/θ)-κ((bκ)/θ)-bκ(κ/θ)=0. Solving for b gives
b=((pκ)/(2κ)).

c) After-tax profits are (1-τ)[pq-w]. Hence, maximizing after-tax profits amounts to
maximizing before-tax profits. Hence, nothing changes to the profit-maximizing level of the
bonus. This can also be verified by doing the analysis. When you take the derivative, the (1-τ)
term drops out. The reason is that the tax applies equally to both the revenues and the
costs. It reduces that marginal (after-tax) revenues. But also the marginal (after-tax) cost.

1.4 (copied from Peter Kuhn’s teaching material)

To find the agent’s optimal effort choice, substitute the contract and production functions into the
agent’s utility function to get U = b(k+E) - E2/2. Maximizing this with respect E to for a given
contract yields E*= b, the same as in our baseline case.  Profits can be written as Π = (1-b)Q =
 (1-b)( E + k).  Plugging E* into this equation, we have Π = (1-b)( b + k) = b – b2 + k - bk.
Differentiating with respect to b gives the first-order condition dΠ/db = 1 – 2b – k =0, and re-arranging
yields b* = (1-k)/2.

Thus, when k=0 (the baseline case in the book), b*=0.5.  This is the case we worked out in Section 3.1.
When k = 0.5, the profit-maximizing commission rate equals 0.25, and when k=1, the profit-
maximizing commission rate is zero.

## Page 4

This example differs from the one in the book because, when k>0, the agent produces some output ‘for
free’, i.e. even when he exerts zero effort.  One interpretation of this is intrinsic motivation:  the agent
likes doing the job so he’ll produce k units of output without incurring any effort costs.  This changes
the principal’s incentives because she now gets k units of output ‘for free’ without incentivizing the
agent, i.e. even when b=0.  Raising b above zero lets the agent keep some of this free output, so the
principal’s gains from raising b are smaller than in the base case when k=0.  This reduces the profit-
maximizing value of b to below 0.5.

When k=1 or higher, the agent produces so much ‘free’, unincentivized output that the principal’s best
policy is not to incentivize the agent at all.  An example might be a charitable organization with highly
motivated volunteers, or an internship situation where the agent is so highly motivated by other factors
(the ability to learn, make connections, and get a good recommendation) that zero cash incentives are
optimal from the principal’s point of view.

Notice that this example contrasts with the “Laffer curve” reasoning underlying Result 3.1 in the text
because we can now get positive output even with zero effort.  Thus a 100% tax rate on the agent –i.e.
b=0, or zero financial rewards for working—can be optimal now.

1.5
a) The worker’s utility can be written as U = w* + be + γe – e2. Taking the derivative to e and
setting equal to zero gives:

b + γ – 2e = 0
Solving yields e = (b + γ)/2.
b) The term may represent the worker’s intrinsic motivation, the worker’s joy from exerting
effort, or the worker’s care about the output of the company. (One of these answers (or
another one that makes sense) suffices.)
c) Using the production function q=e and the optimum effort derived under a), profits can be
written as: Π = (p – b – c)(b + γ)/2 - w*. Taking the derivative to b, setting equal to zero, and
solving gives: b = (p - γ - c)/2. Clearly, when the energy cost c is higher, the company benefits
less from increasing output, as it needs to pay a higher energy cost c for each unit of output.
Hence, it sets a lower bonus.
