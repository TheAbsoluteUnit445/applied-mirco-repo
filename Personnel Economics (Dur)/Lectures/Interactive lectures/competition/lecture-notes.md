- Study sabotage, collusion, risk choice, and unequal contests.

## 2. A tournament rewards relative performance

- The prize depends on rank rather than a fixed output payment.
- Examples include bonuses, promotion slots, and competitive admissions.
- A higher winner–loser pay gap increases the stakes.

Winner: a + S; loser: a

Kuhn20.1PDF364–366. S is the prize spread; a is loser pay. The prize need not equal the winner’s current marginal product.

## 3. Fair and symmetric describe different properties

- Fair: better measured performance wins; ties receive equal chances.
- Symmetric: contestants share relevant ability and cost parameters.
- A fair contest can still be very uneven.

Kuhn20.1 and22.1. These definitions are central to distinguishing biased rules from ability differences.

## 4. Output combines effort, ability, and luck

- Two risk-neutral workers choose effort E₁ and E₂.
- Productivity d is identical; effort costs E²/2.
- The higher measured output wins.

Qᵢ = dEᵢ + εᵢ; C(Eᵢ) = Eᵢ²/2

Kuhn20.1–20.2PDF364–369. Keep expected output dE separate from realized output. Luck shocks are exogenous.

## 5. Effort matters through the probability of winning

- Worker1 wins when d(E₁ − E₂) exceeds relative luck.
- Assume relative luck is uniform on [−R/2, R/2].
- Inside the support, probability rises linearly with the effort gap.

p₁ = 1/2 + αd(E₁ − E₂), α = 1/R

Kuhn20.2. R here is the width of relative luck, not revenue. The formula is local/interior; outside the support probability must be0 or1.

## 6. Move your effort and your rival’s effort

- The line is steep when relative performance is precise.
- Outside the noise support, further effort cannot change the rank.
- Compare own effort with the rival, not with zero.

p₁ = clip[1/2 + αd(E₁ − E₂), 0, 1]

Use the sliders and drag the probability marker. Dashed line marks rival effort. The plotted clipped probability is globally valid; a first-order condition using its slope only applies in its interior.

## 7. Expected utility weighs the prize against effort cost

- Expected pay is a + pᵢS.
- Only the chance of the extra prize depends on effort.
- An extra effort unit costs Eᵢ at the margin.

EUᵢ = a + pᵢS − Eᵢ²/2

Kuhn20.3–20.4PDF369–372. Risk neutrality lets us use expected money minus money-equivalent effort cost.

## 8. Set marginal prize benefit equal to marginal effort cost

- Differentiate expected utility with respect to own effort.
- Inside the uniform-noise region, dpᵢ/dEᵢ = αd.
- The second derivative is −1, so the interior stationary point is a local maximum.

αdS − Eᵢ = 0 ⇒ Eᵢ = αdS

Kuhn20.3. With probability bounds, check boundary/global alternatives too. Constant marginal winning probability is special to uniform relative luck; general models depend on the effort gap.

## 9. A spread of 10 induces effort of 4

- Take α = 0.1, d = 4, S = 10, a = 9.
- Effort: E = 0.1 × 4 × 10 = 4; expected output = 16.
- At equal effort, expected pay = 14; utility = 14 − 8 = 6.

E = 4; p = 1/2; EU = 6

Kuhn20.6PDF373–375. Firm expected profit per worker16−14=2. Equal effort gives each worker a50% chance, although incentives induce substantial effort.

## 10. Prize spreads change optimal effort

- Move S; locate the utility peak.
- Probabilities stay between zero and one.
- Compare the global maximum with the FOC.

Interior E = αdS

Rival effort is fixed in this best-response lab. At kinks or saturation, inspect the true maximum rather than extrapolating the linear FOC. The numerical maximum is found on a fine grid and is labeled approximate.

## 11. Efficient effort maximizes total surplus

- Wages transfer money between employer and workers.
- Each worker produces dE and bears cost E²/2.
- Differentiate surplus, not the wage bill.

TS = d(E₁ + E₂) − (E₁² + E₂²)/2

Kuhn20.4PDF370–372. Assumes no externalities, risk costs, or sabotage. Payments cancel when profit and worker utility are added.

## 12. Choose the spread to implement efficient effort

- Marginal product is d; marginal effort cost is E.
- Efficient effort is E* = d.
- Since E = αdS, the implementing spread is 1/α.

E* = d; S* = 1/α = R

Kuhn20.4–20.5. A less precise contest needs a larger spread in this simple interior implementation. R means the relative-luck range.

## 13. Base pay allocates surplus and secures participation

- At symmetry p = 1/2.
- Require expected utility to meet the outside option Ū.
- A binding participation constraint determines a.

a = Ū − S/2 + E²/2

Kuhn20.4–20.6. a may be negative in the unconstrained model. Limited liability or minimum wages would add constraints; those are extensions, not included in this formula.

## 14. Tournaments and piece rates can implement the same effort

- Piece-rate utility: a₀ + bdE − E²/2.
- The worker chooses E = bd.
- Choose b = αS and adjust base pay to equalize utility.

Piece rate b = 1 and tournament S = 1/α implement E = d

Kuhn20.6. Equivalence depends on risk neutrality and the model’s assumptions; output measurement, strategic effects, and risk can break it.

## 15. Relative performance cancels a common shock

- Suppose Qᵢ = dEᵢ + c + εᵢ.
- A common downturn c lowers both workers’ outputs.
- Their output difference does not contain c.

Q₁ − Q₂ = d(E₁ − E₂) + ε₁ − ε₂

Kuhn20.8PDF380–385. Common-shock insurance is a benefit of relative evaluation; the rival’s idiosyncratic luck adds another source of risk.

## 16. An early promotion buys a chance at later prizes

- A worker values the immediate raise and future opportunities.
- Later stages need incentives even when fewer future rounds remain.
- Large top-level raises can compensate for lost continuation value.

Effective stakes = immediate prize + continuation-value gain

Kuhn20.7PDF375–380. This is a schematic dynamic incentive expression, not a full solved promotion model.

## 17. Comparison groups make relative pay credible

- Broiler producers face shared input and market conditions.
- Relative rewards can filter common shocks.
- Group composition and grower comparability are important design choices.

Kuhn20.9PDF385–387. Connect the evidence to common shocks and selection of comparison pools, rather than assuming all relative contracts work.

## 18. Rank rewards can redirect effort into harming rivals

- Raising own output and lowering a rival’s score can both improve rank.
- Helping a colleague may reduce one’s chance of winning.
- A stronger prize can therefore weaken cooperation.

Rank gap = own output − rival output

Kuhn21.1PDF390–399. Directed sabotage targets competitors likely to beat you. Productive effort and ranking manipulation are different activities.

## 19. Sabotage has a private return and a social cost

- Let productive effort e raise the gap by d·e; sabotage s raises it by s.
- Costs are θe²/2 and κs²/2.
- Interior incentives can induce both activities.

e = αdS/θ; s = αS/κ

Constructed extension, not a formula quoted from Kuhn. Assumes a locally linear winning probability and additive score damage. Sabotage can destroy output or only misreport it; distinguish the two.

## 20. Peer evaluation can become a strategic weapon

- In the envelope experiment, workers could understate rivals’ output.
- Sabotage targeted stronger competitors.
- When sabotage was possible, adding prizes could reduce useful output.

Kuhn21.1; Carpenter et al.2010. Discussed findings are specific to the experiment. Do not interpret every tournament as lowering output.

## 21. A prize can help workers while harming total surplus

- Constructed example: output falls from 100 to 80; r = 2, effort cost v = 0.5 per unit.
- With a piece rate of 1 and expected bonus 3.12: profit change = −23.12.
- Worker utility change = −6.88; total surplus change = −30.

ΔTS = (r − v)(80 − 100) = −30

Uses Kuhn21.1 accounting formulas with illustrative numbers. Worker utility need not rise for arbitrary values; the textbook’s actual experiment had a worker-utility gain. Here it falls, showing the sign depends on parameters.

## 22. Reduce sabotage by changing what is rewarded

- Protect objective records and limit strategic peer scoring.
- Recognize cooperation using credible measures.
- Smaller prize spreads can reduce destructive stakes.

Kuhn21.1. These choices can also weaken productive incentives, so compare the net effect. Peer reports about cooperation can themselves be strategic.

## 23. Workers can agree to reduce effort together

- If everyone slows down, relative rank need not change.
- The prize can remain while collective effort costs fall.
- Each worker still has a temptation to secretly work harder.

Same rank chances + lower costs → incentive to collude

Kuhn21.2PDF399–403. Distinguish cooperation in production from collusion against the employer.

## 24. Monitoring and repeated contact make collusion easier

- Fruit-picker evidence compares relative pay with individual incentives.
- Output restraint was associated with working alongside friends.
- Being able to observe others helped sustain the restraint.

Kuhn21.2; Bandiera et al.2005. The design and monitoring variation help distinguish collusion from altruism. Remedies include reshuffling, larger groups, and confidential performance information.

## 25. Trailing contestants may gamble for a chance to win

- Suppose the mean score gap is Δ and relative noise has standard deviation σ.
- A leader may prefer a safer strategy.
- A trailer may prefer more variance, even if output is not improved.

p(win) = Φ(Δ/σ)

Illustrative normal-noise model for Kuhn21.3PDF404–407. dp/dσ has the opposite sign to Δ. This holds the mean gap fixed; risk actions can also change expected output.

## 26. Move risk and compare a leader with a trailer

- For Δ > 0, increasing σ pushes victory probability down toward 1/2.
- For Δ < 0, increasing σ pushes it up toward 1/2.
- The firm may prefer a different risk choice.

dp/dσ = −φ(Δ/σ)·Δ/σ²

Constructed normal model. Drag σ and adjust the mean gap. The graph does not claim that all workplace risks are normally distributed.

## 27. Incentives depend on how much effort changes the chance to win

- A likely winner can coast; a distant trailer can become discouraged.
- The relevant object is the slope of winning probability.
- Ability differences and biased rules can both shift this slope.

Marginal prize benefit = S·∂p/∂e

Kuhn22.1PDF411–414. With uniform noise the interior slope is constant, so asymmetry need not reduce incentives until a boundary matters. Smooth-noise models clarify the general mechanism.

## 28. A balanced contest has the steepest normal-noise slope

- Let Δ be the performance advantage and h a handicap against the leader.
- A handicap moves the relevant gap toward zero.
- Balanced chances increase local incentives in this model.

p = Φ((Δ − h)/σ); ∂p/∂e = d·φ((Δ − h)/σ)/σ

Illustrative normal-noise extension. Treat Δ as a pre-effort or fixed comparison gap; this graph is a local incentive calculation, not a solved asymmetric equilibrium.

## 29. A superstar can blunt competitors’ incentives

- The golf study discussed by Kuhn links Tiger Woods’s presence to worse performance by rivals.
- The mechanism: the top prize seems less attainable.
- Absences and controls help examine alternative explanations.

Kuhn22.2PDF414–417; Brown2011. The local Markdown result contains an apparent reversed wording; the surrounding discussion supports worse performance when Woods was present. Treat the study as evidence in context, not a universal law.

## 30. Leagues and handicaps can restore competitive stakes

- Leagues group similarly able contestants.
- Handicaps can make an uneven contest more balanced.
- Fair rules are incentive-efficient for equally able contestants in the basic model.

Kuhn22.3PDF417–421. “Fair” here means rules select the best measured performance. A handicap can be useful for incentives while violating that narrow rule definition.

## 31. The best incentive rule may differ from the best selection rule

- A prize contest may aim to raise current output.
- A promotion contest also aims to assign the right person to a future job.
- Separate current achievement, preparation, and underlying potential.

Kuhn22.3–22.4. Handicapping need not imply promoting an unqualified person; rewards and job assignment can be separated.

## 32. Ability learning changes the structure of a promotion ladder

- Survival changes the composition of the contestant pool.
- In Rosen’s example, heterogeneity rises then falls across stages.
- Relative to equal abilities, required raises are larger in the middle.

Kuhn22.4PDF421–425, Result22.6. This is a particular symmetric-ignorance model, not a general rule for every promotion ladder.

## 33. Selection-only contests can favor early leaders

- When managerial attention is scarce, early results summarize information.
- A large later improvement may be required to draw attention to a trailer.
- This differs from balancing a one-shot effort contest.

Kuhn22.4PDF425–427; Meyer1991, Result22.7. Identify the objective and information constraint before applying a fairness result.

## 34. An upset can reveal valuable ability to outside employers

- Mixed pools can create informative wins against strong rivals.
- Workers may exert effort to achieve or prevent an upset.
- Outside wage responses can change early-stage incentives.

Kuhn22.4PDF427–429, Result22.8. Market-based rewards differ from prizes fully controlled by the current employer.

## 35. Seeding changes the option value of reaching the final

- A mixed early pool can leave strong workers facing weaker finalists.
- That can raise the value of winning the first round.
- Higher early effort may outweigh lower effort in an uneven final.

Kuhn22.4PDF429–432, Result22.9; Stracke and Sunde2014. Compare both stages, not only the final.

## 36. Tournament entry reflects beliefs as well as ability

- Perceived relative ability and tastes for competition matter.
- Some people perform better under pressure; others do worse.
- Risk aversion matters through the risks of each available pay scheme.

Kuhn23.1PDF436–439. Common shocks can make relative pay attractive to risk-averse people, while idiosyncratic rival risk can deter entry.

## 37. High confidence can make a risky prize look attractive

- Safe alternative pays 12; tournament pays 30 to a winner and 0 otherwise.
- Ignoring effort differences and risk, entry needs p ≥ 12/30 = 0.4.
- Someone with true p = 0.3 but perceived p = 0.6 may enter and earn less in expectation.

Perceived value = p̂·30; actual value = p·30

Constructed example: perceived18 versus actual9. It separates beliefs from actual performance, holding effort and nonmonetary preferences constant.

## 38. Move perceived success probability and risk aversion

- Compare a safe payoff with a tournament certainty equivalent.
- A larger risk penalty raises the entry threshold.
- Beliefs can induce entry even when actual expected value is low.

CE ≈ p̂S − (ρ/2)·p̂(1 − p̂)S²

Illustrative mean–variance approximation with0/S prizes. ρ is absolute risk aversion; the formula is an approximation, not exact utility for arbitrary risk. Common-shock insurance is omitted here.

## 39. Better tournament output need not mean greater effort

- More able workers may select into higher-prize contests.
- Observed average performance combines selection and incentives.
- Random assignment or within-person comparisons help distinguish them.

Observed performance change = composition + behavior

Kuhn23.1, Leuven et al.2011. This is an organizing decomposition, not an identified equation without a research design.

## 40. Equal task ability can coexist with different entry rates

- Niederle and Vesterlund compared piece rates with tournaments.
- Men and women performed similarly on the task in the discussed experiment.
- Confidence and tastes for competition contributed to different entry choices.

Kuhn23.2PDF439–445; Niederle and Vesterlund2007. Findings concern study populations and settings, not fixed traits of individuals.

## 41. Information and culture can change competition choices

- Accurate relative-performance feedback can encourage underconfident talent.
- Cross-society evidence shows that gender patterns can reverse.
- Choose policies based on the workplace and measured outcomes.

Kuhn23.2. The textbook discusses Wozniak et al.2014 and Gneezy et al.2009. Avoid applying population averages to an individual hiring decision.

## 42. A two-period promotion model adds pride

- Each worker earns W in period1 and W or W + Z in period2.
- Promotion adds nonmonetary pride P. Effort occurs only in period1.
- Cost is θe²/2; outside utility is V each period.

pᵢ = 1/2 + π(eᵢ − eⱼ)

Actual local Final2025Q3, PDF7–8,10points. Revenue per output unit is R in this exam, unlike Kuhn’s noise-range R. Use π for probability sensitivity.

## 43. Pride adds to the effective promotion prize

- There are two base payments W.
- Promotion yields financial Z and pride P with probability p.
- Differentiate with respect to own effort.

EUᵢ = 2W + pᵢ(Z + P) − θeᵢ²/2

e* = π(Z + P)/θ

F25Q3a,2points. First derivativeπ(Z+P)−θe=0; second derivative−θ<0. Linear probability is an interior model.

## 44. At equal effort, solve the participation constraint

- Symmetric effort gives each worker p = 1/2.
- Set expected utility equal to the two-period outside option 2V.
- Solve for the base wage.

2W + (Z + P)/2 − θe²/2 = 2V

W = V − (Z + P)/4 + θe²/4

F25Q3b,3points. Substitute e=π(Z+P)/θ if expressing W only in parameters. Do not use V once when the outside option is available in both periods.

## 45. The employer chooses the financial promotion prize

- Only period1 effort produces output: revenue is 2Re.
- Total financial pay is 4W + Z. Pride is not a cash payment.
- Substitute effort and the binding participation constraint.

Π = 2Re − 4W − Z

Z* = R/π − P; e* = R/θ

F25Q3d,3points. Let A=Z+P: Π=2RπA/θ−π²A²/θ−4V+P; FOC gives A=R/π. This solution assumes an interior feasible contract.

## 46. Check effort, utility, wages, and profit together

- Illustrative values: R=6, π=0.1, θ=2, P=40, V=13.
- Z=20; e=3; W=13−15+4.5=2.5.
- Worker utility=5+30−9=26; profit=36−10−20=6.

EU = 2V = 26; Π = 6

## 47. Pride can save cash but it also induces costly effort

- Holding Z fixed, greater P strengthens the incentive to work.
- It directly raises promotion utility and indirectly raises effort costs.
- If the firm optimizes Z, higher P can replace part of the cash prize.

Interior optimum: dZ*/dP = −1; de*/dP = 0

F25Q3c requires a verbal explanation of both utility and cost channels. The comparative statics after optimizing Z are a separate observation. If Z≥0 binds, the interior result changes.

## 48. Test the limits of the simple model

- Why can a large prize lower output when sabotage is possible?
- Why does a 10-person friendly class support collusion more easily than a 50-person class?
- Why might mixed ability pools improve early effort in a promotion ladder?

Answers: ranking can reward harm and undermine cooperation; monitoring and enforcement are easier in small repeated groups; option value and informative upsets can change early incentives. KuhnDQ21.3 and22.4 motivate the latter prompts.

## 49. Design a contest around its purpose and its risks

- Incentives: prize spread × effect of effort on winning.
- Efficiency: output gains versus effort, risk, and destructive behavior.
- Selection: who enters and who wins are different questions.
- Always state the noise, information, and feasibility assumptions.

Formula checklist: E=αdS; E*=d; S*=1/α; basepay from participation; exam e=π(Z+P)/θ and Z*=R/π−P. Textbook pages and the actual exam are recorded in the source guide.
