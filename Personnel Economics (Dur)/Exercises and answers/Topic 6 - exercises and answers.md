Exercise 6.1

Consider a perfectly competitive economy where workers can choose between two types of
jobs: safe jobs and risky jobs. Safe jobs pay 50.000 euro per year and entail no risk
whatsoever. When taking a risky job, workers have probability p of injury. Worker’s utility
function is:

U = w + g,

where w is the worker’s income and g is the worker’s health. When injured, g = – 100.000.
When not injured, g = 0.

a) Derive the level of the wage firms must offer to attract workers for a risky job.

Suppose p=0.10. At a yearly cost per worker of 7.500 euro, the probability of injury when
having a risky job can be halved.

b) Will firms invest in reducing the probability of injury?

Suppose the government enforces firms to make the investment.

c) What happens with wages of risky jobs and prices of the products workers with risky jobs
produce as a result of the government’s safety regulation?

Suppose the government aims to maximize social welfare.

d) What could be the government’s reason for enforcing firms to invest in safety?

Exercise 6.3
Suppose life consists of two periods of equal length. In the first period, people can either work or
study. In the second period, all people work. People are assumed to care only about the present
value of life-time consumption.
People differ in innate ability a. A person’s wage depends on his ability and on his education. An
individual with ability a who works in both periods earns income w∙a per period. An individual with
ability a who studies in the first period earns nothing in the first period, and income w∙a + p∙a in the
second period, where p > 0. Tuition and other direct cost of studying are equal to t. The per-period
interest rate is equal to r. We also assume that p > (1+r)w.
a) Which people will decide to study in period 1? How does the interest rate affect the decision to
study? How do the other variables affect the decision to study? Give interpretations for your results.
Suppose now that the government imposes a tax of rate τ on labor income. An economist warns that
such a tax may weaken people’s incentive to study.
b) Analyze whether the warning of the economist is justified. Explain your result.
Another economist argues that the distortionary effect of taxes on education decisions can be
nullified by making tuition tax deductible (that is, if somebody has paid amount t tuition fee, then
(s)he receives an amount of τt from the government in period 1).
c) Check whether the economist is right.

Exercise 6.4
A firm employs workers who have productivity 100 per year and are paid a yearly wage of 80. The
firm is contemplating sending the workers to a specialised training, which costs the firm 50 per
worker plus three months absence of each worker. The training increases each worker’s yearly
productivity by 10, starting the year following the training. Workers are identical, except for their
year of retirement, which is denoted by T. The firm maximizes the present value of profits and has no
competitors. The interest rate is r.
a) Which workers should the firm send to the specialised training? Interpret your result.
Suppose now that with probability π, a worker will decide to retire after T – 10 years rather than
after T years. With the remaining probability, the worker will retire after T years.
b) Under which condition should the firm send a worker to the specialised training? Interpret your
result.
The manager wants to reduce the risk of sending workers to the specialised training who know that
they want to retire early. Simply asking the workers about their retirement plans could help, but the
manager fears that not all of the workers will respond honestly as they seem to like to go to the
training resort. The manager therefore thinks about structuring workers’ compensation in such a way
that they reveal their retirement plans.
c) Give a suggestion about how to structure workers’ compensation so that only the workers who do
not intend to retire early choose to go to the training resort.

Exercise 6.5
A profit-maximizing firm has hired a worker for two periods. The worker’s productivity is 70 per
period. The firm pays wage 70 per period to the worker, which is equal to the worker’s outside wage.
Firm-specific training could increase the worker’s productivity by 50 in period 2. The cost of the
training is 30, which includes productivity losses in period 1. The interest rate is zero.
a) Verify that if the worker’s wage remains constant, firm-specific training raises the firm’s profits.
Suppose the firm has invested in firm-specific training of the worker in period 1.
b) What is the maximum wage the firm is willing to pay to the worker in period 2? (In other words:
What is the maximum wage the worker can ask if he can make a take-it-or-leave-it offer to the firm?)
The worker threathens to take another job and leave the firm unless he gets part of the -- in his view
exorbitant -- profits in period 2. The firm and the worker settle at an equal split of the surplus in
period 2.
c) How much does the worker earn in period 2?
d) What would the firm’s decision on firm-specific training be if it had anticipated the worker’s
threath?
e) How can both the firm and the worker be better off, given that the worker always succeeds in
capturing halve of the surplus in period 2?

Answers
6.1
a) 50.000 = w + p(-100.000), and hence w = 50.000 + 100.000p.
b) No. It reduces wage costs from 60.000 to 55.000. But this is not sufficient to make up for
the cost (7.500).
c) Wages will be lower: 55.000 rather than 60.000. Since the economy is perfectly
competitive, profits were zero, and must become zero again. The government regulation
brings a net loss to the firm of 2.500 per worker (see part b)). In the long run, this will imply
exit of firms and hence lower supply of goods, and thus higher prices. In an open economy
with competition from foreign firms, there may be no effect on prices; Instead, production
of these type of goods may completely disappear in this country after introduction of the
safety regulation.
d) Worker’s lack of information about risks or costs or externalities. As an example of the
first, one could assume that workers underestimate the cost of injury (e.g. workers think
that p = 0.10, but the government knows that p is higher; Information provision by the
government may be a better way to deal with the lack of information). An example of the
latter is collectively financed medical costs (But a better way to deal with this problem may
be to condition medical insurance premiums on occupation).

6.2 (partly adapted from Peter Kuhn’s teaching material)
a) Profits = [Q - W]·N(W). Differentiate to W and set equal to zero gives:
- N(W) + [Q – W]·(dN/dW) = 0
Multiply by (w/N):
- W + [Q – W]·(dN/dW)(W/N) = 0
Rewrite
W = [ eta / (1 + eta) ] Q
b) Using the formula w = ηQ/(1+ η), the answers are $18, $16, $12, and $10.  As the labor
supply elasticity rises, Alfredsons experiences a greater and greater loss of workers when it
cuts wages below $20.  Thus, when η = 9, Alfredsons experiences a dramatic loss of workers
when it cuts wage, so it has no choice but to pay workers almost their full productivity (Q-w
= 20-18 =$2).   When η = 1, Alfredsons experiences a much smaller loss of workers when it
cuts its wage, so its profit-maximizing strategy is to keep half of what each worker produces:
(Q-w = 20-18 =$10).

6.3
a) Present value of life-time consumption when not studying = wa + wa/(1+r). Present value of life-
time consumption when studying = – t + (wa+pa)/(1+r).
People study if: – t + (wa+pa)/(1+r) > wa + wa/(1+r). Rewriting yields: a > [(1+r)t]/[p – (1+r)w].
Differentiate to r gives da/dr = {t[p – (1+r)w] + (1+r)tw}/[p – (1+r)w]2 > 0. So, when the interest rate
goes up, less people study. Also note that less people study i) when tuition goes up, ii) when the
return to education p decreases, iii) when the wage rate w (the reward for innate ability) goes up.
b) The economist’s warning can be justified as follows. People study if: – t + (1–τ)(wa+pa)/(1+r) > (1–
τ)wa + (1–τ)wa/(1+r). Rewriting yields: a > [(1+r)t]/{(1–τ)[p – (1+r)w]}. Clearly, when τ increases,
fewer people decide to study. The interpretation of this result follows. A tax on labor income reduces
the benefits from education (the returns to education decrease by τpa) as well as the cost (foregone
labor income decreases by τwa). Since pa>wa, the reduction in the benefits is larger than the
reduction in the cost, and so fewer people decide to study.
c) Yes. People study if: – t(1-τ) + (1–τ)(wa+pa)/(1+r) > (1–τ)wa + (1–τ)wa/(1+r). Rewriting yields: a >
[(1+r)t]/{[p – (1+r)w]}. The tax rate no longer plays a role in study choices. Try to figure out why this is
the case!

The returns to training increase with T. Relatively young workers (those with a high T) should be sent

who know they will not retire after T-10 years will choose to be trained.
