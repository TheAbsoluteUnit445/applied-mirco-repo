## 1. Discrimination: decisions, evidence, remedies

- Explain why equally productive people can be treated differently.
- Separate preferences, information, beliefs, and attention.
- Use evidence to choose a remedy.

A full lecture for Topic 7. Work through the interactive labs before the practice questions. Source: Kuhn ch16, PDF281–301.

## 2. A group difference is a question to investigate

- A raw wage or hiring gap can reflect productivity, job assignment, hours, or discrimination.
- Economic discrimination concerns differential treatment tied to group identity rather than relevant individual productivity.
- Define the decision and the comparison group first.

Kuhn16.1, PDF281–283. Distinguish descriptive disparity from identification of a discriminatory mechanism. An economic definition does not settle a legal or ethical question.

## 3. Follow the entire employment pipeline

- Application → attention → interview → offer → wage → promotion.
- The observed workforce is selected at every stage.
- Equal pay among employees can coexist with unequal access to employment.

Illustrative pipeline extending ch16. Ask which stage is observed and which applicants disappear from the data. Conditioning on being hired can conceal discrimination in hiring.

## 4. Four mechanisms suggest different remedies

- Taste: someone dislikes working with a group.
- Statistical inference: a group proxy substitutes for missing information.
- Biased beliefs: expectations are inaccurate.
- Implicit bias or attention: identity changes processing or judgment.

Kuhn16.1, PDF283–287. Several mechanisms can operate together. This taxonomy organizes an explanation; it is not a diagnostic test by itself.

## 5. A prejudiced employer acts as if hiring costs more

- Productivity v and wage w give financial surplus v − w.
- Taste cost t adds a subjective penalty for one group.
- The employer may reject a profitable hire.

Hire if v − w ≥ t

Illustrative Becker-style taste model, consistent with Kuhn16.1. t is a preference cost in money-equivalent units, not an actual wage payment.

## 6. Taste costs can overturn a profitable hire

- Assume identical productivity across groups.
- At v = 100 and w = 80, financial surplus is 20.
- A taste cost above 20 changes the employer’s decision.

Subjective surplus = 100 − 80 − t

Drag the marked point or use the slider. The line is an illustrative decision model, not estimated data. At t=20 the employer is indifferent.

## 7. Competition pressures employer prejudice

- An unprejudiced employer can hire a rejected productive worker and earn a profit.
- Entry and expansion can shrink an employer-taste wage gap.
- Market power, search frictions, or limited entry weaken this pressure.

Kuhn16.2, PDF287–290. Competition is a conditional mechanism, not a claim that discrimination must disappear.

## 8. Customer and co-worker prejudice can enter revenue

- Customer taste may lower demand when a group member serves the customer.
- Co-worker taste may change retention, morale, or teamwork.
- These effects can make discriminatory behavior privately profitable.

Δ profit = productivity gain − revenue or team loss

Kuhn16.1–16.2. Private profit and social desirability are separate criteria. Do not call all prejudice a purely subjective employer cost.

## 9. Statistical discrimination can use accurate group beliefs

- An employer observes a noisy individual signal and a group prior.
- A relevant prior can affect predicted productivity even without dislike.
- Members can be misclassified because group averages do not describe every person.

Predicted productivity = λ·signal + (1 − λ)·group mean

Illustrative Bayesian linear prediction. Assumes a normal prior and unbiased normal signal with independent noise; λ = prior variance / (prior variance + signal-noise variance). Kuhn16.1, PDF284–286.

## 10. Better signals reduce the weight on a group prior

- Two applicants have the same signal of 80. Group means are 60 and 70.
- At λ = 0.4: predictions are 68 and 74, a gap of 6.
- At λ = 0.9: predictions are 78 and 79, a gap of 1.

Prediction gap = (1 − λ)·10

All numbers constructed for teaching. A better individual signal reduces prior weight under this specific model. This is not a universal prediction for every statistical discrimination model.

## 11. Drag signal reliability toward one

- Both applicants have the same individual signal.
- Increasing reliability moves both predictions toward that signal.
- The group-based prediction gap narrows.

λ ∈ [0,1]

Graph holds the common signal at80 and group means at60 and70. Move λ along the horizontal axis.

## 12. Inaccurate beliefs require a different diagnosis

- A manager may underestimate a group’s ability or commitment.
- Reliable feedback can reveal prediction errors.
- Correction requires the manager to notice and use that feedback.

Kuhn16.1–16.2, PDF285–289. Accurate inference and biased expectations are analytically distinct even if both produce a hiring gap.

## 13. Discrimination can help create the gap it predicts

- If training is less likely to be rewarded, its expected return falls.
- People invest less; later average qualifications differ.
- Employers may then treat the resulting gap as evidence for their initial belief.

Train if p·B ≥ c

Illustrative investment threshold: p is probability that training is rewarded; B is reward; c is cost. Kuhn16.2, PDF288–289. The loop is possible, not inevitable.

## 14. A lower reward probability can discourage training

- Training costs 2; its reward is 10.
- At p = 0.30, net expected return is +1: train.
- At p = 0.10, net expected return is −1: do not train.

p* = c/B = 0.20

Constructed example holding training technology and ability fixed. It isolates an incentive channel; psychological effects are an additional channel.

## 15. Expectations can change performance directly

- Stereotypes and expectations may affect confidence, stress, or effort.
- Pygmalion and Golem effects refer to positive and negative expectation effects.
- Observed performance can therefore be endogenous to treatment.

Kuhn16.2, PDF288–289. Avoid interpreting every later productivity difference as a pre-existing trait.

## 16. An audit experiment holds application quality fixed

- Send comparable applications with randomly assigned identity signals.
- Measure responses such as callbacks.
- Randomization identifies the effect of the identity signal in the tested setting.

Effect = callback rate A − callback rate B

Kuhn16.1, PDF282–286. A name can signal multiple perceived attributes; the treatment effect alone does not uniquely identify taste, beliefs, or attention.

## 17. Percentage points and relative percentages differ

- Illustrative callback rates: 12% versus 8%.
- Absolute difference: 4 percentage points.
- Relative advantage: 4/8 = 50%.

12% − 8% = 4 pp; 12/8 − 1 = 50%

Teaching rates are illustrative. Bertrand and Mullainathan’s name-based audit, discussed by Kuhn, found roughly50% more callbacks for white-sounding names. Do not present these constructed12% and8% rates as their exact estimates.

## 18. Extra résumé information tests a specific explanation

- Randomize résumé quality as well as the identity signal.
- Ask whether employers respond similarly to the same improvement.
- A persistent or widening gap challenges a simple missing-information account.

Kuhn16.1, PDF284–286. In the discussed name audit, improved résumé quality did not eliminate the name gap. This does not rule out every model of statistical discrimination.

## 19. Measure attention before you measure judgment

- Was the application opened? How long was it considered?
- Unequal attention can generate unequal outcomes before any interview.
- Track both process measures and final decisions.

Kuhn16.1, PDF285–286. Attention evidence provides a channel; it need not establish conscious animus.

## 20. Match the intervention to the mechanism

- Taste → accountability and constraints on discretion.
- Poor information → validated individual measures.
- Biased beliefs → calibrated feedback and review.
- Attention bias → structured, consistent processing.

Kuhn16.3, PDF290–295. These are mechanism-based design principles, not guaranteed effects of any named intervention.

## 21. Blind screening removes some signals, not every proxy

- Remove names or other identity cues at an appropriate stage.
- Measure which applicants advance and who is finally hired.
- Effects depend on the context and the stage at which information returns.

Kuhn16.3. Orchestra evidence is discussed alongside mixed results from anonymized applications in other settings. Avoid a universal success claim.

## 22. Removing information can encourage proxy inference

- If relevant individual information disappears, an employer may substitute a group proxy.
- A policy can improve one stage while worsening another.
- Evaluate the causal effect on final opportunities.

Kuhn16.3, historical ban-the-box example. This is an economic mechanism from the textbook, not advice about current law.

## 23. Is an expensive selection test financially worthwhile?

- Test cost c = $100,000 per applicant.
- Success probability p = 0.05; value of a successful hire V = $1,000,000.
- Compute expected value before deciding.

Expected net value = pV − c

KuhnDQ16.1, PDF296. Simplifying assumption: the stated value is net of other hiring costs and unsuccessful candidates add no value.

## 24. Expected value is negative at a 5% success rate

- Expected benefit: 0.05 × $1,000,000 = $50,000.
- Net expected value: $50,000 − $100,000 = −$50,000.
- Break-even success probability: $100,000/$1,000,000 = 10%.

p* = c/V = 0.10

This calculation does not answer ethical or legal questions and does not establish a comparison with another group whose pass rate is unknown.

## 25. Move the pass rate across the break-even point

- Below 10%: testing loses money under these assumptions.
- Above 10%: testing has positive expected value.
- Change test cost to see the threshold move.

EV = p·$1m − c

Drag the point to change p; the second slider changes c. Independent repeated trials imply expected tests per success1/p and expected testing cost per success c/p, not a guarantee of success.

## 26. A cheaper first stage may improve the decision

- At p = 0.05, expected tests per success are 20.
- Expected testing cost per success is $2 million.
- Consider a cheaper screen, but measure its false negatives and selection effects.

Expected cost per success = c/p

KuhnDQ16.1 extension. This expectation assumes independent identical success probabilities and repeated testing until a success.

## 27. Explain the mechanism before proposing a remedy

- A name gap persists after richer résumés. What can you infer?
- A group trains less when promotions are unlikely. Trace the feedback loop.
- Anonymization changes interviews but not final hiring. What must be measured next?

Suggested answers: persistence challenges a specific information account, not all statistical discrimination; lower expected reward reduces investment and can reinforce beliefs; track later-stage decisions and total hiring effects.

- DQ16.1: separate expected profit from fairness and missing evidence.
- DQ16.2: identify when anonymous screening could help or backfire.
- DQ16.3: distinguish individual productivity from team externalities.

KuhnPDF296. For16.3, consider whether a productivity advantage is outweighed by co-worker morale effects; then discuss why a private-profit calculation alone is insufficient for evaluating discrimination.

## 29. Diagnose → identify → intervene → evaluate

- A gap is evidence to investigate, not a complete causal explanation.
- Taste, information, beliefs, and attention require different tests.
- Decisions today can change tomorrow’s productivity.
- Use expected values carefully and report uncertainty.
