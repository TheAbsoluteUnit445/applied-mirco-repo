Exercise 5.1

A stylish restaurant is in need of waiters. Two types of waiters can be hired: people with and
people without a diploma. Waiters with a diploma are much quicker in giving advice and in
serving dinners. If the firm hires waiters with a diploma, it needs two waiters, each of whom
will yield sales of 500 euro per evening. If the firm hires waiters without a diploma, it needs
three waiters, each yielding 300 euro per evening.

a) Suppose a waiter with a diploma costs 100 euro per evening, whereas a waiter without a
diploma costs 25 euro per evening. Which type of worker will be hired by a profit-
maximizing restaurant?

The restaurant wants its waiters to be properly dressed. Suppose the dry-cleaning of working
cloth cost the restaurant 30 euro’s per worker per evening.

b) Which type of worker will be hired by a profit-maximizing restaurant, taking into account
the cost of dry-cleaning working cloth?

c) At what level of cost of dry-cleaning is the restaurant indifferent between the two types of
workers?

Exercise 5.4

A firm can choose between two types of workers: a proven one, who yields output 50 per
period for sure, and a risky worker, who either yields 150 each period, which happens with
probability 1/3, or yields -/-30 each period, which happens with probability 2/3. Assume that a
worker’s wage is independent of type.

a) Suppose the firm operates for one period. Which type of worker should the firm hire, if it
aims to maximize profits?

b) Suppose the firm operates for two periods. It can hire a worker at the beginning of the first
period as well as costlessly replace a worker at the beginning of the second period. Which
type of worker should the firm hire at the beginning of the first period, if it aims to maximize
profits?

c) Consider the same setting as in part b), but suppose now that there is a cost of replacing a
worker at the beginning of the second period, denoted by c. Derive the value of c for which
the firm is indifferent between hiring one or the other type of worker at the beginning of
period 1.

Exercise 5.8

Consider an economy where firms compete for workers, and there is free entry and exit of
firms. Suppose measuring a worker’s output costs c euro per worker per day. Workers differ
in their daily productivity. The least productive worker in the economy has productivity 0, the
most productive has productivity 100. Workers are distributed uniformly over the interval 0 to
100. Firms can either pay a straight salary or offer piece-rate pay. In the latter case, output
must be measured. None of the firms make profit.

a) Suppose measurement cost are zero. Which workers will work at a salary firm?

b) Suppose measurement cost are 10. Which workers will work at a salary firm?

c) Derive the level of measurement cost at which all of the workers will work for straight
salary.

5.4
a) A proven worker (50>30).
b) A risky worker. Expected profits are 1/3 x (150+150) + 2/3 (-30 + 50), which is higher than
50+50. (Use backward induction).
c) c=20. (100 = 1/3 x (150+150) + 2/3 (-30 -c+ 50) gives c=20).

5.5
a) No, all will obtain a degree.
b) Yes, only the high-ability workers will get a degree.

5.6
a) Energetic cost 18/9=2 per unit of output; lazy workers are more expensive per unit of
output.
b) Piece-rate should be higher than or equal to 2 and lower than 2,40.
c) Yes, but only if the firm can ask a fee. A fee of a little over 0,50 works. It implies that lazy
workers can make 5x2,50 – 0,50 <12, whereas energetic workers make 9x2,50 - 0,50 = 22 >
18. Clearly, the fee should not be higher than 4,50.

5.7
W1 < Wu and W2 > [1/ Ps][(1+ Ps) Ws – W1].

5.8

for straight pay. Zero-profit condition implies that pay will be 20 (the average productivity).
This gives all workers with productivity higher than 20 an incentive to work for a piece-rate
firm. And so on…
b) All workers with productivity less than 20. Define q* as the productivity of the indifferent
worker, with all workers having productivity equal to or less than q* willing to work at a
