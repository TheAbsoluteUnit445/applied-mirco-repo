Exercise 8.1

Consider a profit-maiximizing firm which employs two employees. Both employees must at
least work from 9 am til 6 pm. Each employee earns a base salary of 50. The firm introduces a
bonus B so as to induce workers to work longer hours. As the boss has difficulties observing
how long workers actually work (the boss goes home at 6 pm himself), the bonus is
conditional on a worker’s performance. The worker who performs best will receive the bonus.
When the workers perform equally well, each gets the bonus with probability ½. Performance
of a worker increases with his working hours. A worker will decide to work longer hours
when the expected benefits of doing so are equal to or exceed the cost. For each employee, the
cost of working longer hours are as follows:

Working from 6 pm til 8 pm cost 10
Working from 8 pm til 10 pm cost infinitely high

a) Til what time will workers work when the bonus B is equal to 15?

b) What is the minimal level of the bonus at which both workers choose working til 8?

Suppose an additional working hour increases the firm’s revenues by 10.

c) Will the firm implement a bonus?

Exercise 8.3 (adapted from Peter Kuhn’s teaching material)

In several parts of this course, we have studied agent behavior and efficient contracts when
the production process takes the form Q = dE + ε, where E is effort, d is a productivity
parameter, and ε is a mean-zero random variable representing the role of luck.  In doing so we
generally assumed that the only action the agent can take is to choose a level of effort, so that
higher levels of E lead to a higher expected value of output, Q.   In some important contexts,
however, agents can take a second type of action after the contract is signed. This action has
no effect on the expected value of Q, but it increases the variance of Q, by increasing the
variance of ε; in other words this action increases the role of luck in the production process.

a) Explain (in words) why it might be in an agent’s interest to take a costly action that
increases the variance of ε.

b) Describe how this may apply to a fund manager in the financial industry who is rewarded
based on his fund’s relative performance.

Exercise 8.4 (taken from one of the last years’ exams)
Astrid and Bella are sisters. Each of them has a job in which she can freely decide on her
work hours. They earn w per hour. Astrid’s utility function is: whA + μ(whA - whB) – (hA)2
where the first term reflects utility from income and the last term reflects the utility costs
from loss of leisure. The term in the middle reflects that Astrid finds it important how her
income compares to the income of her sister. We assume that μ > 0, and so assume that
Astrid likes it when she earns more than her sister and dislikes it to earn less than her. The
utility function of Bella is whB + μ(whB - whA) – (hB)2. The sisters choose their hours
independently from each other and simultaneously.
a) How many hours will Astrid and Bella choose to work?
b) Assume now that Astrid and Bella jointly decide on their work hours. They choose work
hours so as to maximize the sum of their utilities. How many hours will Astrid and Bella
work?
c) Calculate and compare the utility levels of Astrid and Bella under questions a) and b).

8.3
a) In general, there are two ways an agent can attain a 50% chance of winning a two-person
tournament.  One is by effort, i.e. by matching her opponent’s effort level.  The other is by
luck, i.e. by increasing the role of luck in the contest so much that the winner is determined
solely by luck.  If the second action is less costly to the agent than the first, it will be in her
interest to win this way.  Note that is especially likely for agents who are behind in a contest,
or for agents who have low ability because for them winning by effort may be either very
costly or even impossible.
b) Brown, Harlow and Stark’s study, which is discussed in the book, of mutual fund managers
illustrates this idea. Fund managers who were behind their competitors at mid-year
increased the riskiness of their portfolios in the hopes of doing well on some high-risk stocks
and winning that way.  Notably, they did this by actively selling safe stocks and buying low-
risk stocks, not just by hanging on to stocks that had performed poorly.

w + μw – 2hA = 0. Solving gives hA = ½ (1+μ) w. Likewise for hB.
