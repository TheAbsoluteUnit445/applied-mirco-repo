## Page 7

Government failure
Normative appeal of government intervention can be limited by
problems in collective decision-making: government failures
Four main reasons for government failure:
1.
Imperfect political representation and problems in
aggregating preferences
2.
Lack of information (about individuals’ preferences and
production processes)
3.
Rent-seeking and corruption (‘regulatory capture’)
4.
Limited / misaligned incentives in case of public production
Lack of financial (profit) motive makes public
organisations less responsive to clients’ needs
(for better or worse!)

## Page 17

17
Obvious question: is there a ‘better’ procedure?
Related question: what are ‘reasonable’ criteria for a
collective decision-making procedure?
–
Unrestricted domain (all ind. preferences allowed)
–
Pareto-criterium (If for all individuals X ≽Y, then the
collective ordering should have X ≽Y)
–
Non-dictatorship
–
Transitivity (if X ≽Y and  Y ≽Z, then X ≽Z)
–
Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives
(if X ≽Y with choice set {X,Y}, then X ≽Y in
choice set {X,Y,Z})
Decision-making procedures / rules


<!-- page 18 -->
## Page 18

18
Arrow’s Impossibility Theorem:
It is not possible to find a decision-making
procedure that always fulfils all these criteria.
Note: Theorem does not state that for a given
decision, you cannot find such a procedure.
Simple example: with identical preferences, any rule works
Theorem implies that direct democracy cannot
generally prevent ‘unreasonable’ outcomes

When does majority voting yield ‘stable’ outcome?
Answer: if individual preferences are ‘single-peaked’
For each ind., one optimal outcome and options
‘further away’ from optimum are less and less desirable
– Note: violates first criterium
Outcome of MV: optimum of median voter
– Wins against all other options
Remarkable prediction:
– In a representative democracy with two parties who
maximise prob. of winning elections, both parties
‘compete’ towards optimal policy for median voter
