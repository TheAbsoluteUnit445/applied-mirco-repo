Optimal taxation · 1/15

A tax system must raise money while changing as little behaviour as possible.

Revenue is a transfer to government. Excess burden is the additional loss from trades or work that no longer occur.

The main notes explain both efficiency and equity. Start here, then attempt the practice before opening its solutions.

Optimal taxation · 4/15

The last euro of revenue should have the same excess burden across goods.

MEB is excess burden per additional euro of revenue; εᵢ is compensated demand elasticity and τᵢ its tax rate. Shift revenue raising toward the lower MEB.

Continue until marginal losses match. This is a condition for efficient revenue raising under the model’s assumptions.

Optimal taxation · 6/15

The inverse-elasticity rule has narrow assumptions and an equity cost.

Perfectly elastic supply and independent demands simplify the rule. Use compensated responses for welfare analysis.

Necessities may have low elasticity and be disproportionately important to poorer households. Efficiency alone does not settle tax fairness.

Optimal taxation · 7/15

Corlett–Hague taxation addresses untaxed leisure through related consumption.

Leisure cannot normally be taxed directly. A good consumed together with leisure can indirectly reduce the attraction of leisure.

This requires information about complementarity with leisure. Low own-price elasticity alone does not identify such a good.

Optimal taxation · 8/15

Edgeworth’s equalisation result assumes incomes do not respond to tax.

Let cᵢ be disposable income, U its common utility function, and λ the marginal welfare value of income. Identical diminishing marginal utility and a utilitarian objective favour equal disposable incomes.

The model’s fixed-income assumption removes the work-incentive cost. Its extreme result is not a general recommendation for a 100% tax.

Optimal taxation · 9/15

A linear income tax combines a common marginal rate with a transfer.

Here y is pre-tax income, t the marginal rate and a a uniform transfer. Disposable income is c.

For a > 0 the average rate rises with income despite a constant marginal rate. Higher t reduces the reward for additional earnings; behaviour changes the revenue available for transfers.

Optimal taxation · 10/15

Marginal-cost user fees can give efficient use while leaving a deficit.

A natural monopoly has falling average cost, so marginal cost is below average cost. A marginal-cost price does not cover total cost.

Average-cost pricing covers cost but deters valuable use. Access charges or Ramsey markups can help finance costs, with exclusion and distortion caveats.

Optimal taxation · 11/15

A one-time wealth levy needs credible commitment to stay one-time.

After wealth has been accumulated, taxing it may appear to leave present choices unchanged. The government can nevertheless want to repeat the levy later.

Savers anticipate repetition and change saving beforehand. This time-inconsistency problem makes credibility part of efficient policy design.

Optimal taxation · 12/15

Tax design also depends on the meaning of equality and the cost of enforcement.

Horizontal equity means equal treatment of equals, but equal income, equal earning capacity and equal utility are different definitions.

Administration, compliance and evasion matter. In the book’s fixed-income, risk-neutral evasion model, an interior choice equates marginal tax savings with expected marginal penalties; zero evasion can be optimal.

Optimal taxation · 14/15

The cost of public funds includes the extra distortion from raising revenue.

A project’s benefits must cover its resource costs and the welfare cost of financing it.

Keep tax revenue separate from excess burden. Distributional weights change the welfare comparison when people value income differently.
