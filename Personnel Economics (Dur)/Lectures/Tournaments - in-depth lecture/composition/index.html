Part 1 · The idea20.1

A tournament pays you for your rank, not for how much you produce

APromotion
Two managers compete. The better one gets the VP job and its raise.
BSales bonus

CBroiler growers

Feature 2 · Only the ranking is measured
The firm needs to know who did better, not how much better.

Part 1 · The idea
A new VP's 50% raise is not pay for being VP.
It is the prize that kept every manager below her working hard for years.
So the question of this lecture: can a firm get efficient effort just by choosing two numbers, a base pay a for everyone and a prize spread S for the winner?

Step 2 · How likely?Result 20.1

Only the effort gap matters: the win probability is a tilted ramp

  • Along the diagonal E1​=E2​ the height is always ½.
  • Moving both efforts up together changes nothing: only the gap E1​−E2​ matters.
  • The slope of the ramp is αd. A wider luck range R makes it flatter.

Step 2 · How likely?Fig. 22.1–22.2

Your win chance rises linearly with your effort, between two kinks

  • Below A you cannot win; above B you win for sure. Extra effort there is wasted.
  • Between A and B every unit of effort adds the same αd to your chance.
  • With smooth (normal) luck the line becomes an S-curve, steepest where efforts are equal.

Step 3 · EquilibriumResult 20.21 pt in F25 Q3b

In equilibrium both work equally hard, so luck alone picks the winner

p1​=21​+αd(αdS−αdS)=21​
  • The prize does not exist to find the hardest worker. It exists to make both work hard.
  • Each runs to avoid falling behind (a rat race), and in equilibrium neither gets ahead.
Exam habit
"Workers choose the same effort, so pi​=pj​=21​" was worth 1 point on its own in F25 Q3b. Always write it.
Check yourself
If luck picks the winner anyway, why not just pay both workers a+S/2 for sure?
Answer
Then effort no longer changes pay: ∂EU/∂E=−E, so E=0. The possibility of losing creates the incentive, even though the outcome is a coin flip.

Part 2 · AdvantagesResults 20.9–20.11

Rank pay works when output is hard to measure and promises are hard to trust

1Only a ranking is needed
It is often easy to say who did better, but hard to measure output in units.
2The firm cannot cheat
The prize budget is fixed, so misreporting who won saves the firm nothing.
3No ratchet
When technology raises everyone's output, the prize stays valid. Piece rates must be renegotiated.
4Insurance against common shocks
A shock that hits everyone cancels out of the ranking (next slide). Pay has just two values, so risk can be lower.

Part 2 · Common shocksResult 20.10

A shock that hits both workers cancels out of the ranking

Q1​−Q2​=d(E1​−E2​)+(ε1​−ε2​)
  • Add a common shock c to both outputs: it disappears from the difference.
  • So the winner, and everyone's pay, does not depend on it. A piece rate a+bQ would.
  • Broilers (Kuhn 20.9): a bad batch of chicks hurts every grower equally and costs nobody the bonus.

Part 2 · More playersResult 20.6

Adding rivals for one fixed prize has two opposing effects on effort

1/N effect · effort falls
With more rivals each has a smaller chance of winning, so the prize feels less reachable.
vs
Competition effect · effort rises
You must now beat more people. With one player, promotion is automatic and nobody works.
Net effect: ambiguous (they cancel exactly under uniform luck). A firm can sometimes raise incentives by opening a prize to more people without making it bigger.

Part 3 · SabotageResults 21.1–21.4

In the lab, a prize for the best worker cut real output and total surplus

Carpenter et al. (2010): workers stuffed envelopes and graded each other's work.
1Directed sabotage
Workers understated peers' quality and quantity, but only of peers who were ahead.
2Less real effort
Expecting to be sabotaged, workers put in less effort. Output fell.
3Surplus fell
Profits fell, worker utility rose a little, and total surplus fell.
Exam-style answer
"Why can a bonus for the best worker reduce output?" → It also rewards lowering colleagues' measured output, and workers who expect sabotage gain less from effort.

When you are behind you should gamble; when ahead, play safe

Part 4 · Asymmetric contestsResult 22.1

An uneven contest blunts the incentives of both players

  • Effort is driven by the slope of the win probability, which is steepest when the race is close.
  • Weak player: even maximum effort barely moves her chance off zero.
  • Strong player: wins almost surely anyway.
Result 22.1
For a given prize, both players supply less effort in an uneven contest.

Part 4 · FixesResults 22.3–22.5

Fair rules, leagues and handicaps restore incentives in uneven contests

1Fair rules (equal players)
Profits are highest when the best measured performance wins and the rules are seen to be fair (22.3).
2Leagues
Group workers of similar ability. Everyone has a real chance to win, and a real risk of losing (22.4).
3Handicaps
Make the stronger player beat a point spread. This raises both players' effort at no cost to the firm (22.5).

Part 4 · Concepts

Fair is a property of the rules; symmetric is a property of the players

Fair
The rules: better measured performance wins; ties go by coin flip.
≠
Symmetric
The players: same ability and same costs.
A perfectly fair contest between Tiger Woods and you is still very uneven. A handicap deliberately makes the rules unfair to make the contest more even. The best rule for incentives need not be the best rule for choosing whom to promote.

Practice · 2-sentence answers

Practice 3: three verbal questions in exam style

1Why a bigger prize when measurement is noisy?
When luck matters more, an extra unit of effort raises the win chance by less. To keep marginal benefit equal to marginal cost at the efficient level, the prize must rise.
2Why can a top-performer bonus lower output?
It rewards lowering colleagues' measured output (sabotage) and not helping them. Workers who expect sabotage gain less from effort.
3Why does Z* fall one-for-one with P?
Effort depends only on the total value of promotion, Z + P, which the firm sets at the efficient level. Pride supplies part of it for free.

Every tournament question follows the same six-step recipe

  1. 1
    Expected utility: base + p × prize − effort cost
  2. 2
    FOC in own effort: slope of p × prize = marginal cost
  3. 3
    Symmetry ⇒ equal efforts ⇒ p=21​ (say it!)
  4. 4
    Binding participation constraint ⇒ base pay
  5. 5
    Profit = revenue − all wages; substitute; FOC in the prize
  6. 6
    Interpret: efficient? which channel? feasible?
Traps
  • Outside option over all periods (2V)
  • Count salaries correctly (4W)
  • Pride is not a cash cost
  • R means different things in Kuhn and the exam
  • "Two effects" = two economic channels, no maths in words
  • Probabilities must stay in [0,1]
