## What must be distinguished first

The everyday question is why two people who could do the job equally well might receive different treatment. Kuhn defines employment discrimination as using legally prohibited or morally objectionable criteria in hiring, promotion, wages or layoffs (chapter introduction, PDF p. 281). This is broader than a wage gap. Two groups can earn different wages because they work different hours, have different qualifications or are assigned to different jobs; conversely, equal wages among employees can coexist with discrimination that prevents some candidates from becoming employees.

The chapter describes protected criteria in the United States in its own historical setting. Treat this as the book's context, not a current legal checklist for another country. Here the economics concerns decision rules and their consequences. A profitable rule need not be ethical; an observed statistical relationship need not justify treating an individual according to a group average.

A useful sequence is: **which decision is being studied; what information was available; what relevant characteristics are held constant; which mechanism could explain the difference?** Taste, accurate statistical inference, inaccurate beliefs and unconscious processing can coexist. Neither a raw gap nor a single audit establishes the mechanism automatically.

Exam-ready answer: “A group gap describes an outcome. To identify discrimination in a particular decision, compare otherwise equivalent candidates who differ in the relevant group characteristic. Even then, additional evidence is needed to distinguish dislike, information-based prediction and biased judgment.”

## Detecting unequal treatment: the resume audit

**Question.** How can we compare employers' treatment without comparing applicants who differ in many unobserved ways? Researchers send fictitious applications to real vacancies and randomly assign a characteristic signalling perceived group membership. Random assignment means that, on average, experience, education and other included qualifications do not explain the callback difference. The employer chooses whether to contact the applicant; the researcher changes the signal and records that decision. The causal outcome is a callback, not employment, wages or career progression. [Kuhn 16.1, *Detecting Discrimination in Hiring*, PDF pp. 282–283](<../../Textbooks/Personnel Economics.pdf#page=282>).

The book's example is Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004): applications to sales, administrative support, clerical and customer-service advertisements in **Boston and Chicago newspapers**, with names perceived as white or African American. White-sounding names received **50% more callbacks** than otherwise identical black-sounding names. That is a relative difference, not 50 percentage points, and concerns perceived race conveyed by names. Kuhn reports that the authors ruled out social-class inference as the explanation for their result (PDF pp. 282–283).

To interpret the arithmetic, let \(c_W\) and \(c_B\) denote callback probabilities for the two name treatments. “50% more” means

\[
\frac{c_W-c_B}{c_B}=0.50,
\qquad c_W-c_B=0.50c_B,
\qquad c_W=1.50c_B.
\]

For a **constructed illustration**, if \(c_B=0.08\), then \(c_W=1.50(0.08)=0.12\). The gap is 0.04, or **four percentage points**. It is not a claim that these were the study's original callback rates.

If applications occur in matched pairs at the same vacancy, compare within-vacancy outcomes so vacancy attractiveness does not drive the comparison. Randomize the signal consistently across sufficient vacancies. Avoid interpreting a comparison of actual employees as an audit: workers were selected into employment, and their qualifications and opportunities can differ.

**What does extra resume information reveal?** In the same study, richer resumes described experience, employment gaps, certifications, honours and email availability. If white names merely signalled those particular qualifications, adding them explicitly to both groups should narrow the gap. It did not; the white-name advantage was somewhat larger among high-quality resumes. This weakens that explanation, but does **not** rule out inference about other unmeasured skills. [Kuhn 16.2, PDF p. 285](<../../Textbooks/Personnel Economics.pdf#page=285>).

Exam-ready research design: “Send otherwise equivalent fictitious applications to real vacancies, randomly assigning names perceived as belonging to the two groups. Compare callback rates between the name treatments. This identifies differential treatment in callbacks, while further information or experimental treatments are needed to identify why employers respond differently.”

## Employer tastes: a private preference with a financial cost

**Question.** Can a recruiter reject the financially best candidate because the recruiter dislikes interacting with that group? Yes. Here the relevant assumption is a preference against the group, holding job productivity and wages fixed. The recruiter trades financial profit against this dislike. [Kuhn 16.2–16.3, PDF pp. 283–284 and 287–290](<../../Textbooks/Personnel Economics.pdf#page=283>).

The following is a **teaching formalization**, not a numbered model in chapter 16. Let \(v\) be the worker's output value, \(w\) the wage and \(t\geq0\) the employer's money-equivalent taste cost. Financial profit is \(\Pi=v-w\); the decision-maker's subjective payoff is \(\Pi-t=v-w-t\). If the outside alternative is zero, hiring occurs when

\[
v-w-t\geq0
\iff v-w\geq t.
\]

With constructed values \(v=120,w=90\), the financial surplus is 30. A taste cost above 30 makes the employer reject a profitable worker; at 30 the employer is indifferent. The taste cost is not a wage paid, and it must not be subtracted from financial profit when reporting accounts.

**Why competition can matter.** An unbiased employer can recruit the productive workers passed over by biased firms. If entry and expansion are possible and firms face the same technology and wages, this improves the unbiased firm's profit position. Biased firms may shrink or exit. Kuhn's argument is a tendency and is qualified: recruiter tastes, biased beliefs and unconscious attitudes are likely to reduce profits **in most cases**. It is not a theorem that every observed gap vanishes immediately. Limited entry, imperfect information and slow adjustment can weaken the pressure; those are explanatory qualifications, not additional empirical findings of chapter 16.

Exam-ready answer: “Employer taste discrimination can make a firm pass over productive workers and reduce its financial profits. Unbiased firms can recruit these workers and expand, so competition creates pressure against this form of discrimination. The argument requires the bias to reduce profits and does not automatically cover customers' or coworkers' tastes.”

## Customer and coworker tastes: prejudice can enter actual profits

**Question.** What if the owner has no prejudice but customers or existing employees do? Now the employer may face a revenue or production loss, not just a personal dislike. A superior candidate can still be less profitable if hiring that candidate reduces customer demand, retention, morale or cooperation. [Kuhn 16.2–16.3, PDF pp. 284 and 287–288](<../../Textbooks/Personnel Economics.pdf#page=284>).

Keep the book's examples in their setting: NBA teams in the **1980s** sometimes obtained higher fan revenues by favouring white players over better-performing black players; Kuhn explicitly says this fan discrimination is largely absent today in the book's setting (PDF p. 284). Southern textile-mill owners in the **1950s and 1960s** who hired black workers faced disruption, morale problems and even sabotage from their exclusively white workforces (PDF pp. 287–288). These are historical examples, not claims about all present-day teams or mills.

A **teaching comparison** defines \(G\) as the gain from the better candidate's productive contribution and \(L\) as the resulting loss from customers or coworkers, both relative to hiring the alternative. Then

\[
\Delta\Pi=G-L.
\]

If \(L>G\), catering to prejudice is privately profitable. Competition does not remove a real revenue penalty just because the owner disapproves of its cause. Social welfare additionally concerns excluded workers, equality and effects on other people; the firm's profit calculation does not settle these questions.

Exam-ready answer: “Customer or coworker prejudice can reduce revenues or workplace productivity when a member of the disliked group is hired. A firm may therefore profit from accommodating prejudice even if its owner is unbiased. Competition alone need not eliminate discrimination of this kind.”

## Statistical discrimination: predicting a person from imperfect information

**Question.** What does an employer do when the individual's relevant skill is expensive to assess? A visible group characteristic can become a cheap, imperfect predictor. Kuhn's definition uses **statistically unbiased beliefs** about group differences in average productivity for the particular job. An inaccurate stereotype is instead a biased belief. A group average can be accurate while badly describing a specific applicant. [Kuhn 16.2, PDF pp. 284–286](<../../Textbooks/Personnel Economics.pdf#page=284>).

The book discusses Chinese electronics producers preferring women for assembly jobs and cites average differences in fine motor skills. Retain the qualification in its footnote: direct dexterity testing may be easy, so statistical discrimination need not be the best explanation; wage discrimination or preferences for a gender-homogeneous workforce may also matter (16.3, PDF p. 288, footnote 6). This is an illustration of a possible mechanism, not a uniquely identified explanation of all such hiring.

For intuition, imagine two candidates with the same noisy individual test score. A recruiter who thinks one group's mean is higher may predict different productivity despite having no dislike. More reliable individual information reduces the need for a group proxy. That is why evaluating a relevant skill directly can improve both individual fairness and matching.

An optional **teaching model**, not the chapter's own derivation, makes the idea explicit. Let true productivity be \(q\), group mean \(\mu_g\), and observed signal \(s=q+\varepsilon\). Under independent normal noise with productivity variance \(\sigma_q^2\) and signal-noise variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), the predicted productivity is

\[
\widehat q=\beta s+(1-\beta)\mu_g,
\quad \beta=\frac{\sigma_q^2}{\sigma_q^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2}.
\]

Every quantity is specific to this illustration: \(g\) denotes a group, \(\varepsilon\) is zero-mean measurement error, and \(0\leq\beta\leq1\) measures the weight on the individual signal. For two applicants with equal \(s\) and a common \(\beta\), subtracting predictions gives

\[
\widehat q_A-\widehat q_B
=\beta s+(1-\beta)\mu_A-\beta s-(1-\beta)\mu_B
=(1-\beta)(\mu_A-\mu_B).
\]

As noise approaches zero, \(\beta\to1\) and this prediction difference approaches zero. As individual information becomes very weak, the group mean matters more. This clarifies the information mechanism; normality and these variances are not assumptions the student should import into a chapter-16 verbal question.

Exam-ready answer: “Statistical discrimination uses group membership as an imperfect signal of job productivity when individual information is missing or costly. The group belief may be accurate on average even though particular applicants are misclassified. Better relevant individual information can reduce reliance on group averages.”

## Feedback: discrimination can create the gap it seems to explain

**Question.** If people expect their individual qualifications to be ignored, why pay to acquire them? A group-based decision rule lowers the private return to skill investment. The disfavoured candidate expects no reward for becoming qualified; a favoured candidate guaranteed promotion may also see little gain from extra effort. Thus the shortcut may be profitable now but costly to firms and workers over time. [Kuhn 16.3, PDF pp. 288–290](<../../Textbooks/Personnel Economics.pdf#page=288>).

A **constructed decision rule** illustrates the incentive. Let an investment cost \(C>0\) increase the probability of a rewarding decision by \(\Delta h\), and let the reward be \(V>0\). Invest if

\[
\Delta h\,V-C\geq0.
\]

When the employer actually evaluates improved individual skill, \(\Delta h\) can be positive. If the employer classifies applicants solely by identity or never reads their qualifications, \(\Delta h\) may be near zero. The candidate rationally reduces investment. A subsequent productivity difference may therefore be partly a **consequence** of treatment, not just a pre-existing cause. Persistent group differences require some groups to retain incentives to invest while others do not; if nobody's skills are evaluated, neither group has this investment incentive (PDF p. 288, footnote 7).

Kuhn reports Bartoš et al.'s applications with links to personal profiles in **Germany and the Czech Republic**: employers were less likely to open minority applicants' resumes, specifically Turkish names in Germany and Asian/Roma names in the Czech Republic. If the resume is not read, higher listed qualifications cannot help. Attention is scarce, so the book allows a rational-allocation interpretation; this does not erase the investment consequence (16.2, PDF pp. 285–286 and footnote 5).

The feedback need not operate only through training. Stereotype threat concerns performance disruption when someone fears judgment through a negative stereotype; Pygmalion effects are improvements associated with high expectations, and Golem effects deterioration associated with low expectations. These psychological channels differ from the economic choice to invest (16.3, PDF p. 289).

The **French grocery-chain cashier** study in Kuhn used work schedules assigned independently of origins and manager characteristics. Minority cashiers had more absences, less time at work and slower performance under managers with stronger implicit bias; under unbiased managers they performed better than majority cashiers. This setting supports a treatment-to-performance channel. It is not a resume audit and should not be described as random assignment of fictitious names (PDF p. 289). The Israeli-school example likewise concerns randomly assigned primary-school classes and later educational choices, not hiring.

Exam-ready answer: “When employers judge people by group averages instead of their own skills, acquiring qualifications becomes less rewarding. Reduced investment can then reinforce the group's low average performance and the employer's expectation. Biased treatment can also affect current performance through expectations or anxiety.”

## Remedies should match the mechanism

Kuhn 16.4, *Reducing Bias in Employee Evaluation*, PDF pp. 290–295, considers the decision environment, monitoring and blind recruiting. A remedy changes information or incentives; its effect depends on what was causing the problem. [Read the source](<../../Textbooks/Personnel Economics.pdf#page=290>).

Give recruiters enough quiet time and cognitive resources to assess candidates: fatigue, pressure and rushed decisions encourage shortcuts. The book draws on psychological studies and judicial settings; it suggests similar effects for recruiting, rather than claiming a universally proven hiring intervention. Monitor recruiters using subsequent performance of hires and objective criteria, and consider direct tests or carefully designed selection systems. But measured employee performance itself may be affected by treatment, and replacing discretion with an algorithm does not magically validate the algorithm's inputs.

In U.S. orchestras, screens concealed auditioning musicians' identities. Goldin and Rouse's analysis compared the same musician's success with and without a screen, controlling for individual quality, and found increased female advancement and final-round success (PDF p. 293). This works especially well because judges can hear a relevant skill without seeing identity. A design that hides skill as well as identity sacrifices useful information.

The limits matter. In Swedish public-sector applications, anonymization raised interview rates for women and non-Western applicants, but only women obtained more job offers. In French firms volunteering to trial anonymous applications, minority hiring fell; the book's likely explanation is that those employers had favoured minority candidates when identity was visible. Volunteering firms are a selected setting, so do not generalize this result to every employer (PDF p. 293).

“Ban the box” hides criminal-record information until later in application. The book reports that Doleac and Hansen found reduced employment for **young, low-skilled black and Hispanic men**. The proposed mechanism is substitution toward race as a proxy for the hidden qualification. Hiding a relevant predictor can **increase** statistical discrimination; this differs from hiding identity while preserving a direct skill test. These are the textbook's historical findings, not a current policy assessment (PDF pp. 293–294).

Exam-ready answer: “Blind recruiting can reduce identity-based judgment when relevant performance can still be assessed, as in screened orchestral auditions. But removing useful individual information can make employers rely more on visible group proxies, or can prevent a selected employer from favouring minority applicants. The effect must therefore be evaluated in the actual information and hiring setting.”

## Worked solutions

These book solutions are **independently derived**; chapter 16 provides discussion questions, not an official grading scheme. Suggested concise answers are teaching guidance, not invented exam marks.

**1. Prerequisite.** A 50% relative increase multiplies the starting probability by 1.5; a 50-percentage-point increase adds 0.50. A callback is an invitation to proceed, so an audit of callbacks does not estimate eventual hires or wages.

**2. DQ 16.1: separate expected resources from judgment.** The question asks whether costly individual assessment can make group screening attractive. The model holds pass probability, test cost and net value fixed; the numbers are explicitly hypothetical in the book. Let \(r=0.05\), \(V=1{,}000{,}000\), and \(C=100{,}000\). Per candidate,

\[
E[\text{net value}]=rV-C
=0.05(1{,}000{,}000)-100{,}000
=50{,}000-100{,}000=-50{,}000.
\]

Expected tests per success are \(1/r=20\); expected testing cost per success is \(20(100{,}000)=2{,}000{,}000\), exceeding the stated value. The break-even pass rate satisfies \(rV=C\), hence \(r=C/V=0.10\). Equality implies financial indifference. This assumes constant probabilities, costs and soldier value, enough applicants and no better prescreening technology.

The private resource calculation favours avoiding this testing pool under those assumptions, but blanket exclusion treats successful individuals according to the group average. A normative answer must state the role of equal opportunity and fairness, consider cheaper individual signals, and consider whether the assumed values incorporate all social benefits. Do not substitute a personal view for the arithmetic, or present the hypothetical as real military data.

Concise answer: “The expected net benefit is −\$50,000 per tested candidate, and testing costs \$2 million per successful candidate in expectation. This makes group screening financially attractive under the hypothetical assumptions, but does not settle the fairness of excluding qualified individuals. Cheaper individual assessment and the value placed on equal opportunity could change the policy conclusion.”

**3. DQ 16.3: identify whose taste matters.** This is coworker-taste discrimination, not employer dislike or statistical skill inference. Define \(G\) as the male applicant's productivity advantage and \(L\) as the loss elsewhere in the workforce. Hiring raises profit if \(G>L\), lowers it if \(G<L\), and is financially neutral at equality. The prompt says profits fall, so its maintained assumptions imply the profit-maximizing choice is the alternative candidate. This does not imply ethical justification: accommodation reinforces coworkers' exclusionary preferences and harms a qualified applicant. The same economic comparison applies with genders reversed; any different moral judgment needs an explicit additional value or contextual consideration.

Concise answer: “Given the assumed profit loss, a profit-maximizing firm would decline the best-qualified candidate to accommodate coworker tastes. This illustrates why competition need not remove coworker discrimination. The profit comparison is unchanged if genders are reversed, while ethical evaluation must consider the excluded candidate and whether the firm's response reinforces prejudice.”

**4. DQ 16.2: preserve the productive signal.** A constructed example is an initial assessment of anonymized software work samples using a preannounced rubric. Hide names and photographs, but retain code, task instructions and relevant qualifications. This can limit identity-based screening at that stage; later interviews may reveal identity, and anonymous samples do not identify every relevant skill. It is analogous to the book's audition example, not a documented new empirical result.

**5. Transfer: quantify and qualify.** Initially the percentage-point gap is \(15-10=5\); the relative advantage is \((0.15-0.10)/0.10=0.50=50\%\). With scores, the point gap is \(16-14=2\); the relative advantage is \((0.16-0.14)/0.14\approx0.1429=14.29\%\). The narrowing is consistent with employers using names to infer the skill the added test measures. It does not prove all discrimination was statistical: residual tastes, biased beliefs, other skill inference or different attention could remain. Causal interpretation of the information effect additionally needs random assignment of the added score information to comparable applications, not simply comparison of two unrelated samples.

## Revision targets

Explain all four conscious causes and unconscious factors; design an audit with the correct outcome; state why competition has different implications for different tastes; explain the investment feedback; and attach each remedy to its information mechanism. When a question requests a verbal explanation, give a causal account in a few sentences, not equations translated into words. For a new empirical question, keep the original population, treatment and measured outcome precise.
